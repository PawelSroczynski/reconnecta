## 1. Introduction

This study examines several plots of land north-west of the village of Nadadouro from the perspective of ecological data collection, assessment, and the development of proposals — with particular attention to the question of water.

Aerial images from 2006, 2018, 2022 and 2023 illustrate the considerable dynamics of land use within the study area.

While forestry plantations dominated in 2006, the image taken twelve years later, in 2018, shows sand-cropped fields and freshly regrowing eucalyptus on the south-eastern part of the property. The southern strip of land is likewise free of trees. Four years later, in 2022, heathland species dominate especially on the northern plots. In 2023, the planted forest trees become more prominent again.

Looking at the same aerial images with attention to the month they were taken, it is striking how much moisture the vegetation in the southern section reveals. That a bright green dominates in May 2023 is unsurprising, but even in July 2022 and indeed September 2018 one can see where shallow groundwater shapes the vegetation.

The only constant element in the landscape is the near-natural woodland on the property at its eastern edge. When, within only twenty years, a landscape experiences such intensive interventions — tree planting, felling, ploughing — the consequences are negative not only for biodiversity but also for the water balance of the area.

The northern plot, the southern plot and the strip linking them are to be used primarily for keeping horses, while the residential and utility buildings are to be erected on the eucalyptus plot and partly on the oak plot to the east.

This report has been prepared with that planning in mind, independently of the question of which plots the client will be able to use, from when, and in what form.

## 2. Geography and Landscape

Nadadouro lies in the Portuguese region of Estremadura and belongs to landscape unit L71, the western part of western Estremadura.

This landscape roughly comprises the coastal strip and hinterland between Nazaré in the north and the area around Torres Vedras in the south. Like many Portuguese coastal landscapes it is shaped by geological dynamics and a close interlocking with the Atlantic.

The region forms part of the Lusitanian Basin; the substrate consists predominantly of Jurassic and Cretaceous limestone and sandstone, with Mesozoic sediments widespread. Topographically, this yields a gently undulating hill country, bounded inland by limestone massifs such as the Serra de Montejunto. Even near the coast there is rock — consider Óbidos, picturesquely set on a small limestone massif.

A landscape-defining geological feature is the valley basins formed by the rise of salt diapirs from the substrate. A prominent example is the basin of Caldas da Rainha.

Landscape unit L71 is characterised by high agricultural productivity. The area is known for fruit and viticulture, especially in polyculture — small to medium-sized parcels mixing vegetable cultivation with orchards. Correspondingly, it has a dispersed settlement pattern of many small hamlets, supplemented by the historic centres of Óbidos and Caldas da Rainha.

Noteworthy landscape components are the oak woodland and the depression with wetland remnants. The wood is diverse in its species composition and should be preserved, cared for and further developed as a habitat worthy of protection, to its maximum possible extent. In the wet depression, the spectrum of regionally typical wetland species should be safeguarded.

## 3. Geology

The geology[^1] of the immediate surroundings of Nadadouro is shaped by Jurassic formations, more precisely the Hettangian–Rhaetian with its "marls and limestones of Dagorda". The Rhaetian is the uppermost stage of the Triassic and the Hettangian is the lowermost stage of the Jurassic. The formation therefore marks the transition between these two geological eras some 200 million years ago.

The Dagorda Formation contains massive deposits of gypsum and rock salt. Because salt becomes plastic under pressure, it is precisely this layer that is responsible for the rise of salt diapirs in Estremadura. Without the gliding layer of Dagorda marls and salts, there would be no salt diapirs and consequently no characteristic depressions such as the basin of Caldas da Rainha.

This is highly relevant for the hydrogeology, because the layers often contain impermeable clays and marls, which act as a barrier to groundwater — in combination with the salt content, this often produces brine springs or thermal waters.

In the valleys north-west of Nadadouro one finds the Astian Complex of Nadadouro and Águas Santas, together with Vilafranca strata bearing lignites (brown coal) and diatomites (kieselguhr) at Rio Maior, Óbidos and elsewhere.

These formations — mostly sandy to clayey deposits marking the transition from marine to continental conditions — belong to the more recent geological past, primarily the Pliocene, around 5.3 to 2.6 million years before present. Whereas the Dagorda strata form the deep foundation, these units represent the surface-near fill of depressions formed by the rise of salt diapirs.

Interbedded are Vilafranca strata, typical continental or freshwater deposits accumulating in the sinks created by subsidence above rising salt domes.

Two economically and scientifically important rocks occur in these strata: lignite (brown coal) and diatomite (kieselguhr).

Brown coal was mined at Rio Maior. It originated from deposited organic matter in former swamps and lagoons.

Kieselguhr is a fine-grained, pale deposit formed of the siliceous skeletons of diatoms. It is found in the basins of Rio Maior and Óbidos and points to nutrient-rich still-water bodies.

This geological complex shows that, in the recent geological past, the region was a mosaic of lagoons, lakes and swamps. The diatomite of Rio Maior is, furthermore, an excellent archive of palaeontological data that help us understand what the climate of Portugal looked like a few million years ago.

## 4. Climate

The climate of the region is Mediterranean, with dry, cool summers, rain-rich winters and strong Atlantic influence. The climate is therefore mild and humid, strongly Atlantic in character. High air humidity and moderate temperatures throughout the year are typical. Frost is rare, but the wind often blows strongly. The effective Köppen–Geiger climate classification is **Csb**.

In a Mediterranean climate, summer is marked by a long dry season of more than three months. This dry season coincides with the hot season, when evapotranspiration clearly exceeds precipitation, causing drought.

The mean annual temperature is approximately **21 °C**; at night, temperatures fall on average to about **7 °C** in the colder months and to about **17 °C** in the warmer months. The highest temperature recorded at Nadadouro in recent years was **39 °C** in September 2016. The lowest daytime temperature was measured in February 2016 at **8 °C**.

Nadadouro receives on average **694 mm** of precipitation per year.

## 5. Landscape History

To trace the landscape history of Nadadouro and of the Lagoa de Óbidos one relies primarily on geological studies of sedimentation and on regional historical archives.

[Figure: Lagoa de Óbidos in the geological map of 1959]

In the time of the Lusitanians (roughly the 1st millennium BC), the region looked entirely different. The Lagoa de Óbidos was not a closed lagoon but a deep arm of the sea reaching far inland. Forested ranges of hills enclosed a navigable bay. Inhabitants settled on the heights (such as the nearby Castelo de Óbidos) and used the brackish basin for fishing and salt production. Nadadouro at that time lay directly on a vital waterway.

With the Romans, and later during the Moorish period, the systematic clearing of forests for agriculture began. Through deforestation, more sediment reached the bay via rivers such as the Rio Arnóia.

Even so, the lagoon was deep enough into the 12th century that large ships could sail up to the city walls of Óbidos. Nadadouro was at that time a strategic point for monitoring coastal movements.

In the 16th to 18th centuries, natural silting increased dramatically. Atlantic currents pushed sandbanks across the mouth. The connection to the sea became narrow and unstable. Former port towns lost their access to the sea. The tsunami triggered by the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 is likely to have contributed substantially to the silting of the lagoon. Nadadouro turned from a coastal into an agricultural community as the receding water exposed fertile alluvium.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, the lagoon threatened repeatedly to silt up entirely and turn into a marshland — which favoured malaria epidemics. Channels were dug and the opening to the sea was kept artificially open to ensure water exchange.

The areas around Nadadouro were intensively used for viticulture and orchards, which shaped today's mosaic of fields and small pine groves.

Today, the landscape around Nadadouro is an ecosystem in which tourism, traditional land use and nature conservation are important factors. The Lagoa de Óbidos has been a protected RAMSAR wetland since the beginning of 2025, with great significance as a refuge for migratory birds and a nursery for fish. The greatest problem remains sedimentation: without continual dredging, the lagoon — which has shaped the region of Nadadouro since the days of the Lusitanians — would disappear within a few decades.

### Potential natural vegetation

By "potential natural vegetation" one understands the state of vegetation that would establish itself in a given area under the currently prevailing environmental conditions (climate, soil, water regime) if human influence ceased.

The potential natural vegetation of the study area can be divided into that of the hill location (the plot at the eastern edge and partly the eucalyptus plot) and that of the valley plots on gravels and sands. In the valleys the potential natural vegetation is a cork-oak forest, on the hill the forest of the Portuguese oak.

Lithologically and geologically, the oak groves are associated with soils formed from limestone, marl, clay and dolomite — the so-called Dagorda marls.

### Oak woods (*Arisaro simorrhini-Quercetum broteroi*)

According to Neto et al.[^2], the dominant woods are those of the Portuguese oak (*Quercus faginea*), conditioned by the prevailing basic soils of marl and limestone. These are closed forests over 10 metres tall, in which the Portuguese oak frequently reaches a cover of more than 50 %. Besides the Portuguese oak, the tree layer commonly contains laurel (*Laurus nobilis*), broad-leaved phillyrea (*Phillyrea latifolia*), strawberry tree (*Arbutus unedo*), wild olive (*Olea europaea* var. *sylvestris*) and, rarely, cork oak (*Quercus suber*).

The shrub layer commonly contains laurustinus (*Viburnum tinus*), mastic (*Pistacia lentiscus*), Mediterranean buckthorn (*Rhamnus alaternus*), tree heath (*Erica arborea*), myrtle (*Myrtus communis*), butcher's broom (*Ruscus aculeatus*) and large Mediterranean spurge (*Euphorbia characias*). Near the ground a herb layer develops, dominated by perennials (*Asplenium onopteris*, *Luzula forsteri*, *Arisarum vulgare*, *Carex distachya*, *Iris foetidissima*, *Arum italicum*).

[^2]: Neto, C. et al. 2008: *Carta da Vegetação Natural Potencial de Caldas da Rainha*, Finisterra 43(86):31–51.

Climbing plants are also well represented in this wood: *Hedera hibernica*, *Rubia longifolia*, *Rosa sempervirens*, *Lonicera hispanica*, *Tamus communis*, *Rubus ulmifolius*.

These "Cercais" (oak groves) are commonly found in the Estremenho limestone massif, in the Serra da Arrábida and in the Jurassic and Cretaceous limestones north of Lisbon — always in mesophilic areas. Although in terms of their potential they are not a rare type of forest, well-preserved stands are scarce, which is why they have been designated as sites within the Natura 2000 network. In Annex II of the Habitats Directive, forests with *Quercus broteroi* form a protected, non-priority Natura 2000 habitat (**9240 — Iberian forests of *Quercus faginea* and *Quercus canariensis***).

Of the regressive stages of the *Cercais*, the laurel grove shows the highest degree of originality. It is an almost tree-form, more rarely shrubby, vegetation formation dominated by laurel (*Laurus nobilis*). These trees grow on calcareous soils with edaphic compensation. They do not tolerate extreme drought and are therefore mostly found on shaded slopes, in depressions or in enclosed valleys. In such situations, laurel groves form either an early phase displacing existing forests or a moister fringe to closed woodland.

The laurel groves of the municipality of Caldas da Rainha represent an endemic plant community of Portugal, with their largest distribution here. From a conservation perspective they are of high interest. They are listed in the Natura 2000 network as ***5230 — Laurel forests*** and are regarded as **priority** by virtue of their relict character and the fact that they unite numerous palaeo-subtropical (Tertiary) floristic elements with laurel-like traits — *Prunus lusitanica*, *Phillyrea latifolia*, *Arbutus unedo*, *Laurus nobilis* and *Myrica faya*.

### Cork-oak woods (*Asparago aphyllii-Quercetum suberis*)

Cork-oak woods represent the second most frequent potential vegetation formation in the municipality of Caldas da Rainha. This is due to the extensive occurrence of Plio-Pleistocene sands, sandstones and gravels (mainly from the Astian Complex of Nadadouro and Águas Santas), on which acidophilous sandy soils have formed. Unlike the Portuguese oak, which prefers calcareous soils, the cork oak is calcifuge and acidophilous. Hence potential cork-oak woods occur in association with large tracts of sandy soils of low pH (acid soils), mostly podzolised. Although they potentially occupy a significant share of the Caldas da Rainha municipality, well-preserved cork-oak forests are rare. These are woodland formations often over 10 metres high and extremely dense, dominated in the tree layer by cork oak (*Quercus suber*), strawberry tree (*Arbutus unedo*), broad-leaved phillyrea (*Phillyrea latifolia*), wild olive (*Olea europaea* var. *sylvestris*), Portuguese oak (*Quercus faginea*), Pyrenean oak (*Quercus pyrenaica*) and chestnut (*Castanea sativa*).

The shrub layer is dominated by species such as *Asparagus aphyllus*, *Daphne gnidium*, *Rhamnus alaternus*, *Quercus coccifera*, *Pistacia lentiscus*, *Myrtus communis*, *Osyris alba*, *Ruscus aculeatus*, *Quercus lusitanica*, *Viburnum tinus* and *Erica arborea*. The climbing species present are *Smilax aspera*, *Rubia longifolia*, *Hedera hibernica*, *Rosa sempervirens*, *Lonicera etrusca*, *Lonicera implexa*, *Rubus ulmifolius*, *Tamus communis* and *Lonicera hispanica*.

The herb layer includes *Arisarum vulgare*, *Vinca difformis*, *Asplenium onopteris*, *Deschampsia stricta*, *Carex distachya*, *Scilla monophyllos* and *Luzula forsteri*. The siliceous cork-oak forests of Caldas da Rainha represent a relatively common plant community in central and southern Portugal, particularly on soils formed from Pliocene and Pleistocene sands, sandstones and conglomerates of the Tagus and Sado basins. In Annex II of the Habitats Directive, cork-oak forests are listed as a protected Natura 2000 habitat, though not as a priority habitat (**9330 — *Quercus suber* forests**).

Cork-oak woods are not preserved within the study area, so no Habitats Directive habitat needs to be mapped for them in the habitats section. Areas that in the original natural landscape carried cork woodland are today characterised by the presence of gorse species (*Ulex* spec.).

### Vegetation units currently present on the property

The climax community of the cork-wood areas of the property was once a dense cork-oak forest ecosystem on acidic to lime-free soils of Pliocene sands or sandstones.

The presence here of the Portuguese oak (*Quercus faginea*) attests that this is a particularly well water-supplied, humid variant — frequently grading smoothly into calcareous soils (where the *Arisaro-Quercetum broteroi* would otherwise dominate).

It is an *Asparago aphyllii-Quercetum suberis* cork-oak wood. This plant community represents the thermophilic, oceanically influenced cork-oak woods (*sobreirais*) of central and western Portugal. The diagnostic combination of the namesake climber *Asparagus aphyllus*, *Quercus suber* and a dense, moisture-loving understorey (e.g. *Viburnum tinus*, *Ruscus aculeatus*) is characteristic of this coastal region.

The associated sigmetum (vegetation series) is the *Asparago aphylli-Querceto suberis Sigmetum* (cork-oak vegetation series of western and central Portugal). In the study area, the degraded stage of this vegetation prevails — the gorse heath; phytosociologically, communities of the bell-heather alliance, *Ericenion umbellatae*. This stage of destruction is the result of decades of forestry with maritime pine and eucalyptus on the original cork-wood areas.

The soil of the hill on the eastern edge of the property is calcareous. Hence the oak forest of the *Arisaro-Quercetum broteroi* dominates here. As a forest-edge community, particularly in moister sites, fragments of the strawberry tree–laurel scrub, *Arbuto unedonis-Laurion nobilis*, also occur.

Where the drainage ditch is still influenced by the calcareous soil, a rush-tall-herb community sits along the ditch — the *Juncetum acutiflori-valvati* from the vegetation class *Molinio-Arrhenatheretea*. This vegetation type is endemic to the Estremadura landscape between the Lisbon region and the Mondego.

Communities of grass species of moist, fresh soils — likewise the *Molinio-Arrhenatheretea* mentioned — also form the vegetation of those sites earmarked as wet horse pastures.

### Habitat classifications used

Habitats can be categorised either through the EUNIS system or through the Habitats Directive (Fauna-Flora-Habitats Directive) classification. Whereas EUNIS aims to cover all habitat types existing in EU Member States, the habitats listed in the Habitats Directive are of particular significance for nature conservation. Where they are priority habitat types, their inclusion in a protected area is mandatory.

The EUNIS habitat list (European Nature Information System) is a hierarchical and standardised classification system developed to identify and describe all habitat types in Europe and the surrounding seas. It is managed by the European Environment Agency (EEA) and serves as a "common language" for scientists, conservationists and governments. Rather than each country using different terms for the same landscape, EUNIS provides a code and a precise description that are universally applicable.

The system has been subjected to far-reaching revisions in recent years. The land classification used here is the 2021 edition, including the most recent updates from 2025/2026.

The Habitats Directive 92/43/EEC of 21 May 1992 on the conservation of natural habitats and of wild fauna and flora has as its principal goal contributing to the conservation of natural habitats (Annex I) and of wild animal and plant species (Annex II) considered threatened within the territory of the European Union.

### Summary of habitat types in the study area

In summary, from the perspective of habitat types, the natural forest vegetation of the hill location of the study area (plot at the eastern edge and partly the eucalyptus plot) belongs to the following habitat type:

- **Iberian oak forests of *Quercus faginea* and *Quercus canariensis* (Natura 2000 — 9240)**

> *"Diagnosis: closed, non-hygrophilous forests whose tree layer is dominated by Q. faginea subsp. broteroi (…). They display well-developed strata of lianas, broad-leaved or spiny shrubs and a shade-loving, persistent herb layer."*[^3]

This forest habitat is particularly worthy of protection: the Portuguese guidance[^4] for this habitat type states:

> *"An aspect that influences the management and conservation of this habitat is its frequent relict character and its population and synecological dynamics. The forests of the Portuguese oak (Quercus faginea subsp. broteroi) probably had their greatest territorial extent under earlier, warmer and moister climatic conditions. Today they represent a relict vegetation, presumably less adapted to the current climate, and therefore showing lower competitiveness against the more xerophytic Mediterranean vegetation (sclerophyllous forests and scrub). This fact makes them little resilient and very vulnerable to habitat disturbances, which favour other vegetation types in competition.*
>
> *Aspects of the reproductive biology of this tree considerably hamper the recovery of these forests after disturbance. There is often a lack of seeds and young plants that could secure restoration after clearance or land-use change. In the course of successional processes, one often observes a development towards other forest types (e.g. forests with wild olive, Olea europaea var. sylvestris, on calcareous soils or cork-oak forests, Quercus suber, on siliceous soils).*
>
> *Hence the preservation of this vegetation is inevitably tied to maintaining strict silvicultural conditions ensuring high seed availability and a competitive advantage of the oaks over other trees and shrubs. That is, it is necessary to guarantee abundant regeneration of the oaks within the biotope (mass effect) and to minimise disturbances within the habitat."*

[^3]: http://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/60da003bce5f57b1
[^4]: https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/60da003bce5f57b1

A further habitat type from the Habitats Directive list is present in relict form, evidenced above all by observations of the following plant species:

- *Cicendia filiformis*
- *Carex flacca*
- *Carex paniculata* ssp. *lusitanica*
- *Cheirolophus uliginosus*

Of note are the last two species, which are also considered peat indicators. All these species stand for the Portuguese-endemic habitat type:

- **Rush meadows with *Juncus valvatus* (Natura 2000 — 6410pt4)**

This habitat type occupies poorly drained depressions at the foot of slopes — which is the case in the study area. These micro-wetlands develop in association with the communities of the *Arisaro-Querceto broteroi Sigmetum* (see habitat 9240). They thus appear on soils of basic substrates, where humic acids buffer the lime and the soils show a slightly acid reaction. The eponymous species *Juncus valvatus*, a Portuguese endemic, has not yet been confirmed within the study area but is regularly present in the immediate surroundings.

[Figures: *Carex flacca*; *Cheirolophus uliginosus*]

Bordering this habitat is a wetland habitat with the following species:

*Lythrum junceum*, *Potentilla erecta*, *Rumex palustris*, *Hypericum undulatum*, *Galium palustre*, *Polygonum amphibium*, *Potamogeton polygonifolius*, *Euphorbia hirsuta*, *Sparganium erectum*, *Typha* cf. *dominguensis*, *Juncus effusus*, *Scrophularia auriculata*, *Callitriche* spec., *Euphorbia hirsuta*.

All these species are characteristic of:

- **Persistent hygrophilous tall-herb vegetation on permanently moist soils (Natura 2000 — 6430pt2)**

Many of these species are typical, in the Mediterranean–Atlantic area of Iberia, of riparian fringes and moist depressions that are not permanently deeply flooded.

Areas that in the original natural landscape carried cork woodland are today characterised by the presence of a subspecies of the European gorse (*Ulex europaeus* subsp. *latebracteatus*). This gorse is a nitrogen fixer typical of nutrient-poor, acid soils near the coast.

They are joined by species such as *Viola lactea* and *Simethis mattiazzii*.

Whereas the violet is a specialised species of moist to alternately moist heaths, often colonising the gaps between gorse bushes, *Simethis* is a typical element of the Luso-Atlantic flora, marking the transition from Mediterranean to Atlantic influences. These areas can thus be assigned to the following habitat type:

- **4030 — Dry European heaths (Natura 2000 — 4030)**

In Portugal this habitat type covers a broad spectrum, but the specific combination here points to the Atlantic to sub-Atlantic gorse heaths of the west (**4030pt3**).

This heathland forms part of a climax complex ecologically termed meso-hygrophilous Atlantic heath. In the phytosociological system of Portugal this corresponds with the *Quercetum suberis Sigmetum*, the cork-oak series, whereby the heath represents the stable degraded phase on degraded soils.

- **9330 — Cork-oak forests (Natura 2000 — 9330)**

*Diagnosis: closed-canopy forests dominated by the cork oak (Quercus suber), in part accompanied by other tree species, with a present liana stock, a shrub zone of broad-leaved and/or spiny shrubs, and a shade-loving, well-developed herb layer, hardly or not at all used by humans.*

This habitat type occurs in very small patches on the southern edge of the study area.

### Synthesis

Flora, vegetation and habitats sketch a similar picture to that already described for geology and landscape: the study area shows in miniature what is typical of the landscape of Caldas da Rainha and around the Lagoa de Óbidos — moist (heaths) to very moist depressions (wetlands) alternate with hills of calcareous substrates on which forest relicts have outlasted the centuries.

The find of pondweed *Potamogeton polygonifolius* in the ditch in the south-western part of the study area indicates the high groundwater table — this species can only persist in the long term in near-natural wetlands.

## 7. Disturbance Factors

In this context, "disturbance factors" refers to interventions in the landscape, or to the consequences of such interventions. These can affect the landscape budget (e.g. water), the vegetation, and human uses.

The best-known disturbance factors today are perhaps the invasive alien plant species, several of which have been recorded in the study area — but nowhere yet in an alarmingly broad expansion. Precisely this circumstance should however lead to those few specimens being eradicated as quickly as possible, before — as has happened in so many other places in Portugal — they become an ecological and ultimately also an economic problem in the study area through mass spread.

[Figure: young *Acacia* spec. growth on the property]

All species marked as invasive in the species table should be removed from the area as quickly as possible by suitable means.[^5]

There are however also disturbance factors in the area that are much harder to recognise, above all because they have probably been acting upon the study area — and in particular upon its water regime — since historical times. We refer to the artificial ditch which drains the floodplain in the western part of the study area through a natural depression in the oak-forest hill in the eastern part. And this probably for centuries.

Without this ditch structure, the depression at the foot of the eucalyptus plot would have no outflow. It is therefore to be assumed that, in historical or prehistorical times, this floodplain was a water body that — geologically speaking — was no longer in direct connection with the Lagoa de Óbidos. A motive for draining this floodplain may have been the mining activity around Nadadouro. See the box below for more detail.

[^5]: www.invasoras.pt

A further, only recently inflicted disturbance was the cutting of the understorey on the oak-forest plot. On the neighbouring plot to the north-east (see photo) one can still see how the vegetation should actually look. The intensive cutting back of vegetation leads to the drying out of soils and thus of the woodland vegetation. This produces precisely the opposite of what was intended: greater fire danger. A wood with abundant understorey does contain more fuel — but that fuel is moist, and its structural diversity slows the advance of a fire.

Another intervention was, of course, the cutting of the eucalyptus stand on the eucalyptus plot. There, the growth conditions for the native species already present have changed abruptly: there is now much more radiation in the form of light and warmth.

Experience shows that most species of the existing understorey cope well and rapidly grow into a near-natural pioneer forest. This is particularly visible on the north and north-west sides of the slope, where small oaks and cork oaks are present. It would be very welcome if, when placing buildings and paths, consideration could be given to this comparatively small area.

A particularly heavy, still impending intervention will be the construction of buildings and paths within the planned new project. It is recommended that natural areas worthy of protection be preserved to their maximum extent, since this will ultimately also benefit those who will live at or visit this place in future.

Existing trees — especially the small oaks and cork oaks already growing on the site — grow up very quickly in our experience, soon provide shade and ensure with their root systems that erosion cannot advance and that water is retained near the surface of the soil.

## 8. Recommendations on Water Resources on the Property

Since it cannot at present be precisely established which uses will require which water qualities and quantities, the question of water resources on the property can be treated here only in general terms.

With a view to the planned project, one can note that water is needed:

- for the houses and their occupants,
- for horse-keeping,
- for the garden,
- for the natural vegetation of the site.

Water for these very different uses likewise has a different character — drinking water, thermal water, irrigation water, etc. Consequently, it may also have different qualities, suited to each purpose of use; and it may come from different sources, provided the quality fits the use.

It is therefore additionally conceivable that water be treated and partly reused. This would create an additional water source:

- **recycled wastewater.**

[Figures: two plan representations — one shows the existence of a depression (in blue, upper plan); the other (light-blue line) shows the depression with the water lines (ditches) that converge in this area]

This depression is striking because the ditch cutting through the oak-forest plot is either the artificially created drainage of the depression or at least its artificially strongly deepened drainage. That is, the depression present on the property was once a lake or a marsh area.

It may also have been, in part, a brown-coal mining area — with the depression formed or deepened by the mining itself. Too much water would have hindered the extraction of brown coal, and the ditch (or its deepening) was the solution.

This depression is significant for the question of water resources on the property — and in more than one respect.

1. From a topographic and geological standpoint, this depression is a basin in which rainwater collects. As aerial vegetation images show, some of this water remains close to the surface and gives rise to a corresponding vegetation of moist heath and wetland plants.

2. Some of the water flows off through the ditch in the oak-forest plot to the west. Bearing in mind the interests of the few potentially affected neighbours, this water outflow could be manipulated — it could, for example, be reduced by a weir and thereby the water level on the depression plots could be raised.

3. Should the (ground) water level in the depression be controllable as described under 2., a very good possibility arises for indirectly supplying horse pastures and horticultural areas with water — that is, getting by for large parts of the year, or even the whole year, without additional irrigation.

The near-surface groundwater is certainly not of a quality suited to drinking water or other high-quality uses.

For uses requiring drinking-water quality, connection to the Nadadouro mains is the obvious choice. It can be assumed that the mains already reach the neighbouring plots to the south at the village edge, and that there is probably even a connection obligation. In any case, in the event of touristic use, the developer must reckon with being required to demonstrate drinking-water quality for that touristic use.

With water from the depression and ditch and a connection to the municipal supply, many of the water questions are provisionally answered. In addition, it would be advisable to treat water used in the building by means of constructed wetlands and reuse it — additionally for irrigating tree cultivations. The treatment plants must be securely sealed against the subsoil and the statutory setback distances to water abstraction points (e.g. deep boreholes) must be observed.

The extent to which a borehole tapping deeper aquifers will be necessary can only be clarified when more precise information on the required water quantities is available. The probability of obtaining very mineral-rich water in such a borehole is relatively high, given the hydrogeological conditions of the area. It is therefore advisable, before commissioning a drilling firm, to have a hydrological survey carried out — either confirming this assessment or identifying a drilling location that may be expected to yield drinkable-quality water.

Further information on thermal water is given in the box below.

### Box — Geothermal significance of Nadadouro

Although Nadadouro itself does not have an active thermal spa like Caldas da Rainha, it lies geographically over geological structures that favour the occurrence of mineral and thermal waters.

The region lies within the Lusitanian Basin, shaped by deep geological faults. Nadadouro is in the immediate vicinity of the Lagoa de Óbidos, a transition area in which the contact between different sediment layers allows rainwater to infiltrate, warm at depth, and return to the surface via faults.

Nadadouro forms part of the "Caldas thermal region". Sulphurous water predominantly occurs, known for its therapeutic properties. These waters are classified as hypothermal or mesothermal (between 20 °C and 35 °C at the source). They are rich in sodium chloride and sulphur compounds.

Geothermal use in the region is currently confined mainly to direct application (balneotherapy at the Thermal Hospital of Caldas da Rainha). For decades there have been rumours that construction or sewage boreholes in Foz do Arelho had encountered hot water — but no technical confirmation or commercial exploitation of these occurrences has ever taken place.

There are old folk traditions concerning *"água santa"* from small springs near the cliffs or the lagoon, used by the local population for healing purposes. These rested rather on the purity or mineral composition of the water than on its temperature.

In the municipality of Caldas da Rainha there is, in general, potential for the following applications:

- Climate control of greenhouses or buildings.
- Aquaculture using the constant water temperature.

In the transition area between Nadadouro and Foz do Arelho (close to the Lagoa de Óbidos) lie exploratory boreholes that confirm sodium-chloride water at temperatures between 22 °C and 28 °C. These are however currently classified as "occurrences" rather than "active exploration concessions". The Direção-Geral de Energia e Geologia (DGEG)[^6] distinguishes strictly between **concessões** (concessions — active areas for commercial use, such as the thermal baths of Caldas da Rainha) and **ocorrências** (occurrences — proven resources via boreholes, for which no active use contract currently exists).

This information is held within the framework of "Recursos Geotérmicos de Baixa Entalpia" (low-enthalpy geothermal resources) and of the registers for mineral and thermal waters in Portugal. Specifically, these data are found in the PDM (Plano Diretor Municipal).

[^6]: https://www.dgeg.gov.pt/pt/areas-setoriais/geologia/recursos-hidrogeologicos/exploracao-de-aguas-minerais-naturais/aguas-minerais-naturais/

## 9. Potentials and Resources for a Water Concept

In the following, potentials and resources for a water concept are listed. A much more concrete water concept than this one can be drawn up once plans for construction and land use are available.

**Natural water enrichment.** The existing forest generates water on, and for, the property by natural means, and acts as an important water store on the property. For this reason the existing forest should be preserved, cared for and developed; where possible, the stand should also be extended.

**Natural water storage.** The aim should be to retain as much water as possible on the property. This is important, on the one hand, to enable groundwater recharge; on the other hand, it makes it possible to have directly and indirectly usable water resources available.

**Public water connection.** Given the touristic nature of the project, there is very probably a compulsory connection to the public network. This should be checked before a deep-borehole drilling is commissioned.

**Wells.** Existing wells are an important resource, possibly with better water quality (generally lightly mineralised) than deep boreholes (generally more highly mineralised). It would be important to test any existing wells for water quality and quantity.

A new well should also be considered. To obtain the best foreseeable water quality and the most suitable site on the property, the consultation of experienced hydrogeologists is recommended.

**Deep boreholes.** To obtain the best foreseeable water quality and the most suitable site on the property, the consultation of experienced hydrogeologists is recommended. Deep boreholes generally yield more highly mineralised water.

**Rainwater.** Rainwater can be an important resource for water supply, especially where rain gutters can be provided on the buildings. Problematic under Mediterranean climate conditions, with rain-rich winters and dry summers, is the uneven distribution of precipitation through the year. This leads to considerable quantities accumulating in the winter months, when no consumption for irrigation is normally needed. Storing those quantities can be a challenge.

If space is available to integrate cost-effective, surface-mountable water tanks on the property, rainwater storage can be a sustainable use, since the resource is available free of charge and provides reliable, good water quality.

During the six months of the winter half-year, rainfall exceeds evapotranspiration, so that a water surplus arises and the monthly water balance is in equilibrium.

Over the period from September to May, rainwater can be captured. On an annual balance, in a year with normal rainfall, a storage quantity of up to **670 m³** of rainwater can be amassed. Since the rainwater would also be consumed, the storage size — depending on the intended use and use period — can also be smaller.

The main building has roughly **608 m²**; the **five small houses of 31 m² each** total **155 m²**; and the **three greenhouses of 135, 68 and 96 m²** together total **299 m²**. As the buildings concerned are distributed across the site at different elevation levels, **decentralised** water storage is also feasible.

**Treated wastewater.** Treated wastewater can be an important resource for water supply. Wastewater arises every day in considerable quantity, and its reuse is well suited to the irrigation of fruit-tree cultures or ornamental gardens, all the more so as nutrients remain even after treatment. It is important that this water be used **underground**, in order to exclude direct contact.

Possibly the touristic project entails a compulsory connection to the public sewage network. This should be checked before facilities for wastewater treatment and reuse are commissioned.

### Calculation of daily, monthly and annual quantities

Worked example of water consumption for a Hotel Rural with 20 rooms at 50 % occupancy:

> 360 l/day × 20 guests = **7,200 l/day** = **3,600 l of treated wastewater/day** (50 % occupancy)
>
> = **112,000 l/month = 112 m³/month = 1,344 m³/year**

A water store would probably only need to be able to buffer the quantity of a few days, since irrigation may not be required every day. As an example: a 20 m³ tank could absorb a little under a week's wastewater production. As a rule, however, the water should be used directly and promptly.

During the summer months the treated wastewater can be used for irrigation; in the winter months it would be released unused into the environment.

Generally it must be noted that wastewater-treatment installations must observe a building setback of at least **50 metres** from other water resources (wells, deep boreholes) intended for human use.

### Executive Summary (English translation of the Síntese Executiva)

**1. Context and Land-use Dynamics**

The study analyses several parcels north-west of Nadadouro in order to gather ecological data and design a sustainable development plan focused on *"três pilares: gestão hídrica, renaturalização e criação de cavalos"* — **three pillars: water management, renaturalisation and horse-breeding**. The historical analysis (2006–2023) reveals strong human impact:

- 2006–2018: Transition from intensive silviculture to sand-based agriculture and *Eucalyptus globulus* monocultures.
- 2022–2023: Abandonment of northern areas (emergence of heathland/ecological succession).
- Satellite monitoring indicates the constant presence of shallow groundwater in the south. Only the native woodland to the east remained stable.

**2. Geology, Hydrogeology and Climate**

The site lies within the Lusitanian Basin (Biophysical Unit L71 — Western Estremadura), under strong Atlantic influence.

- Deep substratum: Dagorda Formation (impermeable complex of clays, marls, gypsum and rock salt). Salt mobility (halokinesis) shaped the Caldas da Rainha depression. The clays act as a barrier to water, generating thermal and saline waters in the region.
- Surface layers: Astian and Vilafranca formations (sandy-clayey sediments with lignite and diatomite), testifying to a past of lakes and swamps. The valley records a deep mesothermal aquifer and discharge zones with water almost at the surface.
- Climate (Csb): Mediterranean with strong Atlantic influence (dry/mild summers and mild winters). Mean annual temperature ~21 °C and precipitation 694 mm, with a summer drought exceeding three months.

**3. Flora and Habitats of High Value (Natura 2000 Network)**

Three principal ecosystems were identified on the basis of potential vegetation:

1. **High Zones (Calcareous/Marl):** Portuguese-oak woods (*Arisaro simorrhini-Quercetum broteroi* — Habitat 9240). Relict species of low resilience requiring strict protection. In shaded zones, this evolves into the priority laurel habitat (*5230).
2. **Valley Zones (Acid/Sandy Soils):** Former cork-oak *montados* (*Quercetum suberis*), today wholly degraded. They have given way to Atlantic heaths (Habitat 4030) abundant in gorse (*Ulex europaeus*), *Viola lactea* and *Simethis mattiazzii*.
3. **Wetlands (Water Lines/Ditches):** Rare rush communities (Habitat 6410pt4 with *Juncus valvatus*) and tall riparian vegetation (Habitat 6430pt2), marked by the presence of *Potamogeton polygonifolius*.

**4. Factors of Disturbance and Degradation**

- *Invasives:* Presence of acacias, *Carpobrotus edulis* (Hottentot fig) and pampas grass. Although currently controlled, immediate mechanical/chemical eradication is recommended.
- *Artificial drainage:* A network of historic ditches cuts through the oak hill and artificially drains the floodable valley.
- *Mining past:* This network is associated with the former lignite exploitation (low-quality coal) of the 19th and 20th centuries. The abandonment of the mines (**c. 1945**) left artificial depressions and reliefs today covered by secondary vegetation.
- *Recent interventions:* Drastic mechanical clearance has recently destroyed the essential understorey of the oak grove, affecting the dynamics of natural regeneration.
